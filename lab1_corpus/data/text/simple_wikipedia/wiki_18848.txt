The Supreme Court of the United States is the highest court in the United States of America. Because of this, the Court leads the Judicial Branch of the United States Federal Government. It is the only U.S. court established by the United States Constitution. Its decisions are supposed to be followed by all other courts in the United States. Since 1935, the Court has met in its own building in Washington, D.C.; before that, it met in the United States Capitol.
History
In the 1787 Constitutional Convention, the delegates, or someone appointed by a state to represent that state's interests, debated the separation of powers between the legislative and executive departments.
People, including James Madison, advocated, or argued in favor of, a national judicial authority (the power to enforce the administration of justice). Delegates who did not want a strong central government argued that national laws can be enforced by the states. The delegates compromised by only making a general outline (an overall plan of making something happen) of the judicial authority, giving the federal judicial power to "one supreme court". They did not define the exact powers the Supreme Court had. However, laws from the federal government later defined parts of how the Court works.
Supreme Court justices
Amount of Justices
There are 9 justices on the court now: one Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices. The U.S Constitution does not state how many justices are required to be in the Court, nor does it state a limit on how many justices there should be in the Court. The 9 justice limit was set by the Judiciary Act of 1869, and it was the last of any law passed that set a set amount of justices for the Supreme Court.
Names of Courts
Courts (a set of all justices of the court while one Chief Justice is serving) are unofficially named after the Chief Justice. The current Court is called the "Roberts Court" after Chief Justice John Roberts. The first Court is known as the "Jay Court" after John Jay.
Justices Serving
All justices serve for life unless they retire, resign, die, or get impeached. If a justice retires, they can be asked to serve as a judge on a Federal Court of Appeals.
When justices leave the court, new justices are nominated (picked) by the President of the United States. To apply checks and balances, they must be approved by the United States Senate.
Process of getting a new Justice
The Constitution does not set any requirements for who serve as a justice of the Supreme Court, so a president many nominate anyone to serve and the Senate may not limit who the president chooses. However, the Senate may prevent a candidate from being a Supreme Court justice through rejection, but these events are not common. A president can remove a nomination before the Senate votes to confirm the nominee (the person being nominated) or not. In addition, the Senate may fail to vote on a nomination, causing the nomination to expire (reach an end date) and no longer be voted on.
Once the Senate approves the nomination, the president must sign a commission (document to give a person an official duty) and sign it before the upcoming justice can be a part of the Supreme Court. The upcoming justice must take two oaths before becoming a part of the Supreme Court.
Process of Supreme Court Cases
The Supreme Court chooses which cases it will decide on, by choosing to give a writ of certiorari or not. Almost 7,000 people, known as petitioners, ask the Supreme Court to decide their cases every year, but the court only gives a writ to about 100 or less.
For the Supreme Court to decide a case, the case usually must be about:
Federal law
The Constitution of the United States
Disagreements between U.S states or the residents of that state
Another court's decision that is different from what the Supreme Court has decided on a similar case.
Introducing a new case
Cases must first be decided by a federal district court and a federal court of appeals or by a state supreme court. The person who lost in the case can request the Supreme Court to look at a case for a final chance at appealing a case.
After that, the Supreme Court can choose not to decide a case for any reason. Cases about disagreements between states or their residents sometimes can only be decided by the Supreme Court, but those are rare.
