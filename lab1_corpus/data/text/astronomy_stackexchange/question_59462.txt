Have we found any exoplanets that have an unusual amount of a reactive gas in their atmosphere?
As far as we know, free oxygen is an extremely reactive gas that cannot exist in large quantities for an extended period of time. In fact, if all plants stopped photosynthesising, our oxygen supply would run out in just $400,000$ years just from respiration. And yet, oxygen constitutes about $23\%$ of our atmosphere by mass, which means our atmosphere possesses $1.18\times10^{18} \, \mathrm{kg}$ of oxygen, which means, that at sea level, the partial pressure of oxygen is $21.5 \, \mathrm{kPa}$. So, as a thought experiment, if some alien astronomers did a spectroscopy of Earth, they would immediately know that it was populated by life (if not sentient life), judging by the sheer amount of oxygen in its atmosphere.
Note that I'm not talking about the percentage of a reactive gas in an atmosphere, as this meaning can be ambiguous. Ganymede's atmosphere is made up of nearly pure oxygen, yet if some astronaut decided to take off his spacesuit, he would suffocate and freeze, because Ganymede's "atmosphere" is practically a vacuum. Similarly, Venus's atmosphere contains only $3.5\%$ nitrogen, yet the partial pressure of nitrogen at Venus's surface is around $ 322 \, \mathrm{kPa}$, around 3 times the atmospheric pressure of Earth at sea level.
This brings me to this question:
Supposing we found a planet, that had a thick atmosphere, that happened to have $40\%$ fluorine gas in its atmosphere. Now of course, fluorine is so reactive it can set water on fire and even react with noble gases, and yet this planet had a copious amount of free fluorine in its atmosphere.
Of course, no known chemical reaction cannot be generating THAT much fluorine in the atmosphere, thus it must be getting replenished by some form of life on the surface analog using an energy source, say light from its parent star, or geothermal heat, to create food and liberate fluorine gas as a byproduct, similar to how cyanobacteria and plants on Earth release oxygen as a byproduct of photosynthesis.
The question is:
Have we ever detected any exoplanet, which upon spectroscopy or a similar method, has been revealed that large amounts of a reactive gas (oxygen/fluorine/chlorine etc) in its atmosphere exist, that cannot exist under natural conditions?
I am not stating that the presence of reactive gases on an exoplanet necessitates the presence of life, I am asking if we have found any exoplanets that possess such an atmosphere.
And when I mean large amount, I am talking about a $>10\%$ concentration of a reactive gas, and I am mostly restricting this search to Super-Earths and Earth-sized exoplanets, because, larger planets are more likely to possess a thicker atmosphere than a smaller planet (with a few exceptions).
