What exactly is the observable universe?
After having searched for it, read the Wikipedia and Britannica entries, and asked AI, I still don't quite get how the observable universe is defined. In particular, if the universe was compressed into a single point at the big bang, why isn't the observable universe infinitely large?
I don't have any problem with the visible universe, which consists of the points in space from which the cosmic microwave background radiation has had enough time to reach us. The recombination event, at which the CMB was emitted, forms the temporal boundary to which we look back in the definition of the visible universe.
As far as I understand, the equivalent temporal boundary with regard to the definition of the observable universe is the big bang itself. But at the big bang, all of space was compressed into a single point. So light emitted from any point of space at the big bang would have had time to reach us, as all points of space were the same point. If the big bang singularity is the temporal limit for defining the observable universe, the observable universe should be infinitely large.
That is obviously not the consensus, so what am I missing here?
Note: I understand that the big bang singularity is an outcome of the current best theory on the development of the universe, and many astronomers don't believe it actually existed as the theory describes but instead that this indicates the limit of what our current theories can tell us. I don't think this is relevant to this question, as the notion of 'observable universe' also follows from the current theory. So any discussion can be limited to working within the current theory and leave the validity of current theory to other places.
