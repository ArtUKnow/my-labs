Do exoplanet studies provide evidence that their orbital axes align with the galactic axis, as expected from conservation of angular momentum?
Like death and taxes, conservation of angular momentum is inevitable.
The angular momentum of the protogalactic cloud provided spin for the Milky Way. Presumably both the spin axis and total momentum were conserved in this process. Similarly, protostellar clouds acquire their angular momentum from their parent galaxy and this is transferred to the planetary systems which subsequently form.
By this line of reasoning, the spin axis of planetary systems should, on average, align with the galactic spin axis. Any individual planetary system can deviate from this scheme due to angular momentum redistribution from magnetic braking, jets and disc viscosity. But since the total angular momentum must be conserved, a sufficiently large sample of exoplanetary systems should demonstrate this expected correlation.
An example is our solar system. The majority of planets rotate and orbit prograde. But Venus and Uranus rotate retrograde. Similarly, most moons orbit prograde but 4 of Jupiter's moons, and one each of Saturn's and Neptune's moons orbit retrograde. On a micro scale angular momentum is "scrambled" but on a macro scale it conserves the momentum of the proto solar disk.
My understanding is that the exoplanetary evidence, to date, has failed to demonstrate my expected correlation. But only a small portion of exoplanetary systems have had their axial orientation determined.
What is the sample size threshold needed, and have we reached that level?
