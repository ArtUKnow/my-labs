Could there be exoplanets made primarily of non-water volatiles?
In our solar system, everything past the asteroid belt, barring the gas giants, Io, and Europa, seem to have crusts and mantles, if not more than that, made purely of liquid water/water ice. Every. Single. Thing. From the smallest comet to the largest moon of Jupiter. In the most extreme case, Saturn’s moon Tethys is only 6% rock and 94% water ice. Its other moon, Titan, where the landscape of ice is a stage for eerily Earth-like geological processes to occur, exemplifies this. In the outer solar system, water ice takes the role that rock does on Earth. Now this is because water is just… everywhere in the cosmos, right?
Now the most common volatile substances in the universe is hydrogen, which is why our solar system has entire worlds made of this, but in gaseous form, as they can’t stay solid at any realistic temperature you could hope to find in space. So, water is the next most abundant, right?
No. Actually, while water is fairly abundant, carbon monoxide is the next most abundant substance after hydrogen. A lot of volatiles have lower boiling points than hydrogen, but not so low as to be impossible to find in solid form. For example, Pluto has oodles of (surface) nitrogen ice. There are tons of different volatiles that could make a planet in solid form, like water does.
But water is everywhere, so the most likely candidate (but not the only one) that could make up a large fraction of a planet’s mass, like it does with Titan, is CO. Carbon monoxide. This has a boiling point similar to nitrogen, so anything colder than that could exist as CO ice. In our Solar System, this is about the temperature at Saturn’s distance from the Sun. Anything farther than that could become a world made of frozen CO. And worlds that had enough mass to hold onto CO in gaseous form could end up like equivalents of lava planets or steam worlds in that the materials that make up the planet begin to melt, but the body’s own self-gravitation holds it together. This would be a truly exotic place, and which might actually be. But again, as I mentioned, other compositions are also possibilities. So could there truly be exoplanets with such exotic compositions (since we can’t figure out their compositions directly)? Or is this not plausible for some reason.
