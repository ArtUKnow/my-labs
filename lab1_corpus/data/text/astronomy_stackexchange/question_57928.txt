How can a planet's perihelion be raised during migration?
I've been reading about the hypothetical Planet Nine, but there are two passages in the Wikipedia article that puzzle me:
Several possible origins for Planet Nine have been examined, including its ejection from the neighborhood of the known giant planets, capture from another star, and in situ formation. In their initial article, Batygin and Brown proposed that Planet Nine formed closer to the Sun and was ejected into a distant eccentric orbit following a close encounter with Jupiter or Saturn during the nebular epoch. Then, either the gravity of a nearby star or drag from the gaseous remnants of the Solar nebula reduced the eccentricity of its orbit. This process raised its perihelion, leaving it in a very wide but stable orbit beyond the influence of the other planets.
It's easy to see how an encounter with another planet can make a planet's orbit more eccentric, raising the aphelion. I'm having more trouble understanding how the perihelion can get raised to circularise an orbit far from the Sun.
It's hard to say the gravity of another star couldn't do that, but it's an explanation that's very convenient for the theory.
However, I don't see how physical friction with the remaining gas of the solar nebula would do it. That will remove momentum and kinetic energy, but those need to be added to circularise an orbit.
Dynamical friction from a massive belt of planetesimals also could have enabled Planet Nine's capture into a stable orbit. Recent models propose that a 60–130 ME disk of planetesimals could have formed as the gas was cleared from the outer parts of the proto-planetary disk. As Planet Nine passed through this disk its gravity would alter the paths of the individual objects in a way that reduced Planet Nine's velocity relative to it. This would lower the eccentricity of Planet Nine and stabilize its orbit.
The same problem seems to apply to dynamical friction. Again, it reduces momentum and kinetic energy.
What am I missing?
