Is an ancient Roman lance a viable model for space junk harpoons?
Space junk is widely recognized as a significant problem. Although currently launched LEO satellites include end-of-life disposal plans, there is a large mass of older junk which does not.
From What is the optimum space junk to target for deorbiting?
This Japanese study identifies the orbital bands which are at the
highest risk of creating an exponential collision cascade and model
how many large debris objects need to be removed to negate the cascade
risk.
https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/tstj/7/ists26/7_ists26_Pr_2_7/_pdf#:~:text=Electrodynamic%20tether%20is%20promising%20as,from%20crowded%20regions%20are%20proposed.
They identify these characteristics of optimal removal targets:
Large size (>0.5m^2) since they are the biggest sources of collision fragments which lead to cascades.
Crowded orbital bands :
Sun synchronous orbits 700-1000km altitude and 98-100 inclination
900-1000km at 83* inclination
1400-1500km altitude at 74*, 83* and 52* inclination
Lower orbits tend to clear themselves from atmospheric drag.
GSO satellites are at relatively low risk.
There are 400 objects which meet the above criteria. If 25% of these
objects were de-orbited, it would negate the risk of a collision
cascade.
Proposals have been made to actively de-orbit the larger pieces of debris by rendezvous and grappling such as the ClearSpace-1 https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ClearSpace-1
Grappling a potentially spinning piece of debris is problematic, especially if angular momentum is transferred to the intervening spacecraft.
One proposed solution is a harpoon.
The demonstrated design used a hinged barb design, similar to a SCUBA spear gun but with a shock absorber and barb release mechanism. Note that in the video, no barbs deployed after the target was penetrated.
This particular design proposes to tow the debris to lower orbit. A more economical mission design would attach a drogue which would use drag to lower the debris altitude, leaving the harpoon-bearing satellite to rendezvous with another piece of debris.
An alternate design to the shock absorber/barb harpoon is the ancient Roman pilum. They were javelins, headed with a triangular sharp iron point, about eleven inches long. There was also a soft iron shaft which was designed to bend after the head penetrated through the opponent's shield. Once they had pierced the shield and the shaft bent, they were almost impossible to draw out.
[![Pilum, without wooden shaft https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilum][1]][1]
The pilum, which was standard issue for Roman foot soldiers for centuries, evolved to optimize its function. This function was to penetrate the opponent’s shield and stay there, making the shield cumbersome and useless in close combat.

I should be clear that the holding mechanism is not the point wedging into the wood, but the bent soft shaft which prevents the triangular point from pulling back through the small hole it has made.
