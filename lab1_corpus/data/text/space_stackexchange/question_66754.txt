Is it possible for Thin Film Isotope Nuclear Rockets to have an Isp over a million seconds?
Thin Film Isotope Nuclear Rockets (TFINER) sound intriguing: cold nuclear rockets (with no moving parts) capable of interstellar velocity. According to https://www.nasa.gov/general/thin-film-isotope-nuclear-engine-rocket , the concept has been awarded Phase 1 funding in NASA’s NIAC.
So yes, “it is a thing”.
The article describes the deceptively simple concept: capture the momentum of radioactive decay products.
…manufacture thin sheets of a radioactive isotope and directly use the
momentum of its decay products to generate thrust. The baseline design
is a ~10-micron thick Thorium-228 radioisotope film which undergoes
alpha decay with a halflife of 1.9 years. The subsequent decay chain
cascade produces daughter products with four additional alpha
emissions that have half-lives between 300ns and 3 days. Thrust is
produced when one side of the thin film is coated with a ~50-micron
thick absorber that captures forward emissions
… 30kg of radioisotope (comparable to that launched on previous
missions) spread over ~250 m^2 of area would provide more than 150
km/sec of delta-V to a 30 kg payload.
Note that 150km/sec is almost 10x the solar escape velocity from an 1AU solar orbit Why is it easier to escape the solar system than get to Mercury or the Sun?
