For asteroid redirection to avoid Earth collision, is the direction of the thrust relevant? Will any push work if it's big enough?
For a near Earth asteroid (NEA) to collide with Earth, the two orbits (Earth and Asteroid) must cross. But also, both bodies must be at the crossing point at the same time.
This is best illustrated by the “Constant Bearing, Decreasing Range” strategy (CBDR) used by mariners to avoid collision. CBDR is also used in Proportional Navigation in missiles such as the AIM-9 Sidewinder Air-to-Air missile

In the CBDR strategy, the target is held at a constant bearing (direction) which assures intersecting trajectories. This means any change in either direction or velocity will avert the collision. It doesn’t matter which direction you push the asteroid.
This is important since a “slow push” soft lander could only land at a pole if the asteroid is spinning. This means the lander’s thrust direction is limited to the spin axis. It can choose how hard to push, but not the direction.
And for an impacter, orbital mechanics places severe limits on the angle of intersection of the two trajectories. It can decide how hard to hit, but not the direction..
So, is it fair to say that to redirect a NEA on collision course, “Just give it a shove. Never mind which way.” ?
Of course, since the target (Earth) is not a point, the shove must be big enough to miss the entire Earth, not just displace the impact site.
As pointed out in the comments, a random push may possibly result in an Earth intercept on a subsequent orbit of the asteroid. It would be interesting to produce a “Pork Chop Plot” of the mission. The axes are the delta-v and the bearing of the thrust. The contours are the distance at closest approach.
