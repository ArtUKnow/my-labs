How would a spacecraft figure out its own orbit, without help from earth?
I was looking into this as a tangent while reading about Orbital Determination, but I can't seem to find any literature on the subject. I am trying to make my own solution but can't quite seem to figure out the numerical approximations, so am throwing this out to see if someone else has an idea.
Imagine you are a space probe, you are on a mission to some planet or moon deep in the outer solar system, when suddenly the DSN network goes down. Without it you are unable to know your exact location, and thus don't know your orbit.
How would you figure out your orbit, and more specifically, could you do it with observations only?
You see, If you have a powerful radar rangefinder you can find your precise position relative to the object your orbiting, and then you can use lambert's problem to figure out the orbit. But that requires the aforementioned radar rangefinder, or something similar, and relies on it being in range and accurate enough to work. The method used to observe orbits from earth is Gauss's method, but that doesn't work in this case if we're observing from the object. It should in principle be possible to figure it out using only four observations and applying Keplers 2nd law, but that method has some nasty Kepler equations thrown in, and I at least can't get the numerical solution to converge.
Anyone who knows of a method, or at least a good place to keep reading?
