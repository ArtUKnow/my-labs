Rotating multi-spoked electrodynamic tether propulsion systems using capacitors to create alternating current in the tether. Possible?
Electromagnetic tethers promise to provide propellant-free solar energy propulsion. Could capacitors on the ends of rotating tethers solve some of their design challenges?
Electrodynamic tethers https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamic_tether are long conductors which interact with the Earth’s magnetic field in orbit. Since this is a conductor moving in a magnetic field, a voltage is created in the conductor. If the ends of the conductor are electrically connected through the ionosphere, a current can flow in the conductor. This requires an electron collector at one end of the tether and an electron emitter (such as a hollow cathode) at the other.
This current, in turn, can generate a Lorentz force which alters the trajectory of the conductor (and any satellite attached to it).
The Lorentz force can potentially be used to deorbit satellites. In contrast, if electrical power (for instance, from solar panels) is fed into the conductor to reverse the current direction, the Lorentz force can, in theory, be used to raise the satellite’s orbit. This effect is of interest because no propellant is expended. If the tether is to function for both raising and lowering the orbit, each tether terminus must have the capability to function as both a collector and emitter of electrons. https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120015031/downloads/20120015031.pdf
Electrodynamic tethers face a number of challenges. Long conductors, up to tens of kilometers in length, are required. This creates significant tension on the conductor due to tidal forces. These tidal forces hold the conductor radial to the Earth’s center, restricting the orientation of the Lorentz force. The long single tether is susceptible to micro meteor damage,
I would like to propose an alternative: a rotating tether with a capacitor at each end. The capacitor would store charge for a half rotation instead of discharging it to the ionosphere. As the tether rotates, it produces alternating current in the tether in phase with its rotation. Or at least it is alternating in the rotating frame of reference of the tether, but direct in the inertial frame of reference of the Earth.
The tether length would be shorter than a non-rotating tether, its length being limited by centrifugal force rather than tidal forces. There could be a multiplicity of tethers radiating from a hub satellite which would carry solar panels, control electronics etc.
The tips of the tethers could be electrically connected as well. This would give control over the phase angle of current in the spoke conductors, potentially biasing the net Lorentz force in a chosen direction for thrust vectoring. And it could potentially alter angular momentum without thrusters or reaction wheels.
Potentially this design could provide solar powered propellant-less thrust and maneuvering in LEO.
