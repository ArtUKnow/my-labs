Are de-orbiting Starlink satellites a danger to commercial airliners, as FAA has told Congress?
Starlink satellites deorbit at the end of their service live. Once the constellation is fully populated, there will be an average of one an hour "timing out" and deorbiting. https://www.cbc.ca/news/science/satellites-polluting-atmosphere-1.7239899
Many sources claim Starlinks are "demisable" (=burn up completely), but the FAA disagrees. The altitude of peak heating and maxQ do not necessarily correlate with the altitude at which fragments burn into harmlessly small fragments. Do fragments of a hazardous size penetrate to the cruise altitude of commercial airliners? Th FAA's report to congress says, "Yes,they do." https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/Report_to_Congress_Reentry_Disposal_of_Satellites.pdf
Based on conservative assumptions, a fragment of 300 grams or greater
may cause casualties to aircraft occupants ... roughly 1.65 million people in the air around the
globe ... people in aircraft derive their vulnerability from debris
that either penetrates the fuselage and directly impacts an occupant
or causes an uncontrolled landing of the aircraft. This debris can be
fairly small ... threshold for aircraft downing objects is roughly 300
grams ... the SpaceX spacecraft could each produce three pieces of
debris of 300 grams ...
Payload reentries are expected to continue to grow to roughly ten
thousand per year if the Starlink constellation's operational lifetime
follows the announced operational lifetimes of 5years. Risks to people
on the ground and in the air will continue to grow accordingly.
The space industry has not yet collectively met the 90 percent success
rate for satellite post-mission disposal, and even at the 90 percent
rate, over 5,000 satellites could fail in orbit and randomly reenter.
Reentry debris from random reentering objects has differing ballistic
coefficients and masses. This causes the reentering debris to fall in
a very long reentry footprint.
Most reentry breakup events occur between 80 kilometers and 70
kilometers in altitude. Fairly quickly after a violent breakup, pieces
cool down and the trajectory becomes nearly vertical as the objects'
forward velocity rapidly decreases. There is no difference in the size
of debris that passes through navigable airspace and debris that
strikes the ground.
The Aerospace technical report estimated that Starlink spacecraft
could create three objects each that could cause an aircraft downing
incident.
Since over 85 percent of the expected 2035 risk to people on the
ground and aviation from reentering debris is a result of FAA licensed
launches of Starlink satellites, it would be worthwhile to
definitively evaluate if any debris from random atmospheric reentry of
Starlink spacecraft survives reentry.
