Would we be able to identify cell material from Europa?
This question is inspired by a research article that details how scientists were able to identify cell material within a single ice grain through a process similar to that of the SUrface Dust Analyzer (SUDA) on NASA's Europa Clipper.
The results of the experiment show that signatures of bacteria potentially embedded in ice grains and emitted via an ocean world plume would be readily detectable using impact ionization mass spectrometry. The authors assert that although an extraterrestrial biosphere might use different biochemistry, it is logical to assume an aqueous-based ecosystem with access to molecular building blocks common in our Solar System would likely use and modify the concentrations of those molecules in ways that would deviate from an abiotic system.
My hypothetical question is as follows: if Europa Clipper flies through a plume and SUDA indeed picks up cell material inside an ice grain, and data on the mass spectrum graph is similar to that below, would we be able to correctly consider (not necessarily with absolute certainty) that the sample came from cell material? Obviously further study would be necessary to be certain, but would the likelihood of the results stemming from abiotic/prebiotic processes be low enough that it becomes reasonable to suggest that SUDA did, in fact, detect evidence of life? If not, what kind of conditions would need to be realized before such a possibility is considered? I know Europa Clipper isn't a life detection mission; I am simply curious about how scientists would go about confirming the existence of extraterrestrial microorganisms if we get the clues.
