@Woody's comment under How does one "mothball" a spacecraft, and then how does one "un-mothball" it later? points to an application where napthalene ($\require{mhchem}\ce{C_10H_{8}}$, the traditional ingredient of mothballs) was used as a solid source of gas for a "hot gas thruster".
The comment links to Australian National University's December 9, 2021 Moth balls thrust satellites into space (also archived):
Naphthalene is ideal because when it is heated it goes straight from solid to gas, with no liquid sloshing about in the thruster," he said.
"It is cheap, non-corrosive and easily available.
[...]The Bogong thruster, heated at about 70 degrees Celsius, turns solid naphthalene to a gas, which is then further heated during its exit from the thruster nozzle to provide the rocket propulsion.
quoting "ANU PhD scholar Mr Dimitrios Tsifakis ". It's described as a viable alternative to ion thrusters for small spacecraft:
The simple design uses more propellant than the plasma thruster but uses very little complicated electronics so that weight can be used to increase the propellant carried.
The article goes on to quote others from the ANU Space Plasma, Power and Propulsion laboratory, and the Project leader Professor Rod Boswell, from Boswell Technologies.
While it is written before launch, the thruster was developed and tested in an appropriate vacuum chamber, so the Isp should be known. So I'd like to ask:
