A Mars Sample Return Misson Profile
The first and second modules are both launched to Earth's Orbit. Their iodine ion engines get them on their way to Mars. The first module is in a free return trajectory. The second module is in a trajectory for a hard landing.
And so they finally reach Mars.
The first module coasts on the Martian atmosphere: it is aerobraking to buy time. An aerogel shield in its front captures some particles.
Meanwhile, the second module proceeds to aerobrake more agressively, but still has a much higher speed: it is well within the atmosphere and falling. As it falls it performs a manouver combining a skycrane descent and a mid-air retrieval of samples¹ [from a rover, a drone or a tethered balloon]. Finally, it imparts a sub-orbital ballistic trajectory to the samples.
Having executed this manouvers, the second module is discarded. It falls and crashes as the samples rise to the high martian atmosphere and hit the aerogel shield of the first module so they are captured.
[The aerogel is a huge, lightweight, and effective shield to capture the canister. As an additional feature, it will capture some atmospheric particles.]
The second module now has the samples and still is on a free return trajectory. Its iodine ion engines accelerate the inevitable return.
Back at Earth's orbit, it is captured by a Dragon capsule. The aerogel shield is collected and brought to Earth by the capsule as downweight.
Additionally, we can put a new aerogel shield and a new block of propellant to have the second module execute another mission; and repeat the process a few times over. After a few more flights, the whole second module can be brought back to be reconditioned. When it can not be further reused, it could be spent as the propulsive stage of a second module.
Lastly, this mission profile can be tested - for much cheaper - around the Earth and around the Moon before being executed in Mars.
¹The samples will be inside a case so they are not dispersed.
