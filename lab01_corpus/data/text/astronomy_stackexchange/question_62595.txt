Murray and Dermott Exercise 1.6: should that be 30 ratios or 29?
This follows on from my earlier question Solar System Dynamics: how does a 4:2 orbital resonance differ from 2:1?, and it makes sense only to those who have read at least Chapter 1 of Murray & Dermott.
Exercise 1.6 is essentially a replication of Roy and Ovenden On the Occurrence of Commensurable Mean Motions in the Solar System, 1954, which tries to fit ratios of periods with fractions $\frac{i}{j}$, where $i<j\le 7$. They argue that there are more simple integer ratios than we'd expect if the periods had been assigned at random. A follow up paper argues that gravitational effects have shepherded the planets/satellites into the simple ratios.
The exercise requires the calculation of near resonances between planets (including the one that Mike Brown killed), and also between prograde satellites of Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, subject to a number of restrictions on the size and eccentricity of satellites. M&D get 30 acceptable pairs of orbits: I get 29 pairs, spanning these ratios: 1:2, 3:4, 2:7, 1:5, 3:7, 1:4, 5:7, 2:3, 1:7, 2:5, 1:3, 4:7, and 3:5. M&D require that each ratio be within $0.5(\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{7})$ of an integer ratio $\frac{i_1}{i_2}$, where $i_1<i_2\le 7$.
I checked the printed text against the errata, which allowed me to increase the number of pairs from my original 25 to 29. I have also found that I can get 30 pairs by fudging the $0.5(\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{7})$ limit slightly: increasing it by a factor of 1.01 added one more ratio, 1:6, Saturn-Neptune. I have done a manual calculation using orbital data from Wikipedia: Saturn-Neptune appears to be outside the limit specified in the Exercise. (I suspect that I'm dealing with roundoff).
(The Exercise asks me to calculate the probability that the number of pairs arises by chance, so 29 versus 30 pairs is unlikely to matter, as long as I haven't misunderstood the whole thing and got 29 by chance).
My data comes from Appendix A of Murray & Dermott. Since Table A-2 doesn't include orbital periods, I have used Kepler's 3rd Law to calculate them.
Has anyone else tried this problem? If so, how many pairs did you get?
