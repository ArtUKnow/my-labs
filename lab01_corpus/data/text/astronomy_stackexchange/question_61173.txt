Cepheids/supernova vs redshift (for estimating distances)
(Note: original title didn't include the "distances" qualification)
I'm following this story of expansion of the universe from an amateur perspective, recently reading this article (a)
Relevant quote from article:
they used three independent methods. The first uses a type of star
known as a Cepheid variable star, which varies predictably in its
brightness over time. The second method is known as the "Tip of the
Red Giant Branch," and uses the fact that low-mass stars reach a fixed
upper limit to their brightness.
The third, and newest, method
employs a type of star called carbon stars, which have consistent
colors and brightness in the near-infrared spectrum of light.
So there are three methods reported in the article and I was surprised to learn that none are based on spectrography (redshift and absorption lines).
So why are these traditional methods preferred to redshift?
Is redshift measurement not reliable enough?
My common sense tells me that all these methods based on "brightness" must be very subjective, especially very faint light coming from great distances and using long exposure and stacking.
There seem to be some evidence of bias and variability / unpredictability, e.g this Type Ia supernova luminosity differences but I'm not sure how largely accepted these are nor whether the brightness methods become less and less reliable with distance.
