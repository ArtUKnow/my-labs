Long-term averaged distance between two planets
I am interested in long-term averages of mutual planet distances in the solar system.
Once the Kepler orbits of two planets $i = 1,2$ are known, the distance $d_{12}$ between them can be calculated using
$$ r_i = \frac{p_i}{1 + e_i \, \cos(\theta_i - \theta_{i,0})} $$
$$ \mathbf{e}_i = ( \cos(\theta_i - \theta_{i,0}), \sin(\theta_i - \theta_{i,0}) ) $$
$$ \mathbf{r}_i = r_i \, \mathbf{e}_i $$
$$ \mathbf{e}_2 \to \mathbf{R}_{12} \, \mathbf{e}_2 $$
where the matrix $\mathbf{R}_{12}$ describes the time-independent rotation of the major axis of planet $2$ w.r.t. planet $1$ in some reference frame.
One finds
$$ d_{12}^2 = (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2)^2 = r_1^2 + r_2^2 - 2 \, r_1 \, r_2 \, \mathbf{e}_1 \mathbf{R}_{12} \mathbf{e}_2 $$
For the approximation of circular orbits, and using
$$ r_i(t) = a_i = \text{const} $$
$$ \theta_i(t) = \omega_i t - \theta_{i,0} $$
$$ \omega_{12} = \omega_{2} - \omega_{1} $$
$$ \mathbf{e}_1 \mathbf{R}_{12} \mathbf{e}_2 = \cos\omega_{12}(t-t_0) $$
this simplifies to
$$ d_{12}^2(t) = a_1^2 + a_2^2 - 2 \, a_1 \, a_2 \, \cos\omega_{12}(t-t_0) $$
So the long-term average
$$ \langle d_{12} \rangle = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T dt \, d_{12}(t) $$
can be calculated analytically *).
My question **) is if there is an analytical expression for an approximation that takes both the eccentricity of orbits incl. $\dot{\theta}_i \neq \text{const} $ as well as the relative rotation of major axes $ \mathbf{e}_1 \mathbf{R}_{12} \mathbf{e}_2 = \cos\gamma $ into account, i.e. that goes beyond the approximation (*).
A rough idea is to approximate for each planet
$$ r(t) = \frac{p}{1 + e \cos\theta(t)} = p \, \left( 1 - e \cos\theta + e^2 \cos^2\theta + \ldots\right)$$
$$ \theta(t) = \omega t + c_1 \, e \, \sin(\omega t) + c_2 \, e^2 \, \sin(2 \, \omega t) + \ldots $$
$$ \cos\theta(t) = \cos \left[\omega t + c_1 \, e \, \sin(\omega t) + c_2 \, e^2 \, \sin(2 \, \omega t) + \ldots \right] = \ldots $$
for each planet.
Last but not least one must do that for the square root ...
*) One uses
$$ \phi = \omega \, t; \qquad d\phi = \omega \, dt $$
$$ a_{12} = \frac{2a_1 a_2}{a_1^2 + a_2^2} $$
$$ k_{12}^2 = \frac{2a_{12}}{1 - a_{12}} $$
$$ 1 - a_{12} \, \cos\phi = (1 - a_{12}) \left(1 + k_{12}^2 \, \sin^2\frac{\phi}{2} \right) $$
to rewrite
$$ \langle d_{12} \rangle = \sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2} \, \frac{2}{\pi} \sqrt{1 - a_{12}} \int_0^{\pi} d\phi \, \sqrt{1 + k_{12}^2 \, \sin^2\frac{\phi}{2}} $$
For the integral one finds the complete elliptic integral of the second kind
$$ \int_0^{\pi} d\phi \, \sqrt{1 + k_{12}^2 \, \sin^2\frac{\phi}{2}} = E(-k_{12}^2) $$
For two planets with $a = a_1 < a_2 = A$ and defining $x = a/A$ this can be simplified to
$$ k = \frac{4x}{(1 - x)^2} $$
$$ \langle d \rangle = \frac{2A}{\pi} \, (1 - x) \, E(-k^2) $$
**) I know that this may not be realistic due to secular variations; let's neglect that.
