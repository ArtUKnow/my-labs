How to understand radiation 'flux' as compared to the flux in an electric field?
I'm working on Section 2.3 from Sara Seager's Exoplanets Atmosphere, and went across this definition for radiation flux:
$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf x, \nu,t) = \int_{\Omega}I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat{n}},\nu,t)\mathbf{\hat{n}} d\Omega$. Notice how here the specific intensity $I(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat{n}},\nu,t)$ is a scalar and that the 'flux' $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf x, \nu,t)$ is a vector.
This differs from the 'flux' I was used to in electromagnetism and vector analysis, which is defined by a surface integral like $\Phi = \oint \mathbf{E}d\mathbf{a}$. And the RHS would be a point multiplication which produces a scalar flux $\Phi$, instead of a scalar.
Now this difference in definition produces an interesting result: the net flux for an isotropic radiation surface element is zero. I was very surprised to find it at first, but this conclusion is confirmed by various sources, including exercise problem 2 in the same chapter in Exoplanets Atmosphere, section 5 in this Max Planck Institute lecture note https://www.mpi-hd.mpg.de/personalhomes/frieger/HEA3.pdf, and page 4 in this book https://crossfield.ku.edu/A391_2020A/Rybicki_Ch1-firsthalf_Radiation.pdf. These sources often interpret the result as 'there is as much energy crossing surface element $dA$ in its normal direction $\mathbf{\hat n}$ as in the $-\mathbf{\hat n}$ direction'.
It's hard to comprehend the conclusion if you borrow intuition from the electric field. The electric flux surrounding an isolated charge that produces isotropic line fields is definitely not zero, and a non-zero flux means that something is giving off electric field in all directions. That's what the Gaussian theorem is all about. Similarly, for an isotropic radiation source, I can't stop feeling that 'something is giving off energy in this surface element'. Yet the net radiation flux is zero, and the sources' interpretation suggests that energy is neither lost nor gained.
So the analogy between the radiation field and the electric field is broken... but why? Mathematically speaking, how are these two fields different? (Why is the Gaussian theorem not applicable to radiation?) And physically speaking, how do you understand the statement that an isotropic radiating source is not losing energy? Is there a better intuition than the electric field to relate the radiation field to?
For the physical side, this discussion https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/is-the-net-flux-from-an-isotropic-source-zero.483418/ differentiates an 'isotropic emitter' from 'a field point in isotropic radiation'. Nevertheless, it's still hard to convince myself that even 'a field point in isotropic radiation' is not losing energy.
