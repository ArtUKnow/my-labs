Why is the 2,308-year Saros 42→118 eclipse-path recurrence so much more exact than neighboring recurrences?
I searched the NASA Five Millennium Catalog of Solar Eclipses for central eclipses with unusually similar computed paths.
My filters were:
central eclipse types T, Tm, H, H2, or H3;
$\lvert\Delta\gamma\rvert\leq0.02$;
$\lvert\Delta\text{magnitude}\rvert\leq0.01$;
$\lvert\Delta\text{central duration}\rvert\leq0.5$ minutes;
distance between the catalogued points of greatest eclipse no more than 250 km, calculated using GeographicLib/Karney.
The standout result was a run of seven consecutive total eclipses in Saros 42 that closely match seven consecutive total eclipses in Saros 118. (The third pair was missed in my initial scan because its greatest-eclipse points were about 252 km apart, just 2 km beyond my 250 km cutoff.)

One answer identified the interval as
$$
28{,}546\ \text{synodic months}
=76\ \text{Inex}+6\ \text{Saros},
$$
which is exactly twice the catalogued but poorly documented cycle called Unnamed (1154):
$$
14{,}273\ \text{lunations}
=38\ \text{Inex}+3\ \text{Saros}
\approx421{,}490.1\ \text{days}.
$$
That arithmetic is clearly relevant, but it does not appear to explain why the Saros 42→118 matches are so exceptionally exact.
The 1,154-year intermediate eclipses
The eclipses one Unnamed (1154) interval after the Saros 42 eclipses are all annular eclipses in Saros 80:
$$
42\rightarrow80\rightarrow118.
$$
For these seven cases, the sequence is therefore
$$
T\rightarrow A\rightarrow T.
$$
Extending the same Saros-number progression gives
$$
4\rightarrow42\rightarrow80\rightarrow118\rightarrow156,
$$
or locally:
$$
A\rightarrow T\rightarrow A\rightarrow T\rightarrow A.
$$
This is consistent with the 1,154-year interval being near a half-integer number of anomalistic months: it tends to exchange a larger apparent Moon with a smaller one, producing an approximate total/annular alternation.
However, the doubled 2,308-year recurrence does not work equally well for the neighboring annular pairs.

Saros 4→80 paths are separated by thousands of kilometres and increasingly diverge in latitude.

The $76I+6S$ relationship therefore provides the organizational framework, but Saros 42→118 is a much more exact local realization than the immediately preceding or following doubled recurrences.
Extending the recurrence far beyond the NASA catalogue
I subsequently extended the calculation using Swiss Ephemeris with the DE431 ephemeris, rather than extrapolating from the NASA catalogue.
I followed the same $+76$ Saros-number progression through 12 successive doubled relationships, covering roughly 27,800 years. For each relationship I compared the same seven corresponding eclipses, giving 84 eclipse-pair comparisons in total.

This suggests that Saros 42→118 is not simply "what a $76I+6S$ recurrence looks like." It appears to be a sharp minimum in the recurrence error.
I am more cautious about terrestrial longitude for the most remote epochs because longitude depends on UT1 and therefore on the adopted $\Delta T$ model. However, the same sharp minimum appears in eclipse-geometric quantities that do not depend merely on rotating the Earth beneath the shadow.
Why the existing answers seem incomplete
The first answer correctly identified the $38I+3S$ 1,154-year cycle and showed that my interval is its double. But that alone does not explain why only one doubled recurrence is nearly exact.
The second answer added an important point: my 842,979.6-day values are in TT, whereas terrestrial longitude depends on UT1. For the Saros 42→118 pairs, the UT1 intervals are extremely close to whole numbers of days. That plausibly explains their unusually good longitude agreement.
However, that still seems incomplete. A nearly integral number of UT1 days does not by itself explain why the same pairs also reproduce gamma, magnitude, path width, and duration so closely, while the adjacent and more distant $+76$ relationships do not.
In other words:
the known Saros–Inex combination explains why a recurrence exists at approximately 2,308 years;
the UT1 calculation helps explain why the Saros 42→118 paths recover nearly the same terrestrial longitude;
neither by itself seems to explain why several largely independent residuals become unusually small at the same point in the longer sequence.
The extended calculation also changes the question somewhat. Saros 42→118 is clearly exceptional within the interval I tested. What I do not understand is the larger structure that produces that exception.
Question
What is the correct dynamical interpretation of this apparent minimum in the $76I+6S$ recurrence error?
In particular, is Saros 42→118 best understood as:
A unique or effectively unique secular coincidence, where slowly changing lunar and terrestrial parameters happen to make the residual vector pass unusually close to zero only once;
A local minimum in a much longer quasi-periodic or beat pattern, so that similarly close $76I+6S$ recurrences should eventually appear again much farther forward or backward in time; or
Part of a genuine higher-order eclipse recurrence, representable by some larger combination of synodic, draconic, anomalistic, tropical, or other relevant periods that predicts when these unusually accurate 2,308-year matches recur?
More specifically, what combination of nodal, anomalistic, seasonal, and Earth-rotation residuals causes Saros 42→118 to be dramatically more exact than the surrounding $+76$ relationships?
And if this is a local minimum in a longer oscillation, what quantity should be tracked to identify that envelope? For example, should one examine the accumulated residuals in draconic, anomalistic, and tropical phase separately as a function of successive $76I+6S$ steps?
Finally, is roughly 27,800 years of successive $+76$ comparisons enough to say anything meaningful about whether this minimum is isolated, or would the relevant beat periods require a substantially longer integration?
