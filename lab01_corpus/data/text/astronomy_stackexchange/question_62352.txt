Are the most massive stars still expected to shed the entire H envelope through winds?
For a while, it has been believed that massive stars shed their envelope through winds. However, it seems that some stuff might have changed:
Binary interactions, including RLOF, appear to be the norm in massive star evolution rather than the exception.
Mass loss rates were overestimated.
There are a lot of models that use ad hoc increase in mass loss rates when the stellar surface helium abundance exceeds a threshold. I can’t think of a reason for this, and it makes much more sense to me for the high mass loss of Wolf-Rayet stars to be for different reasons (such as luminosity near the Eddington limit).
Even the most massive main sequence star known (R136a1) still has an optically thin wind, and while it is losing a lot of mass, I’m not sure if it will be able to get rid of its whole envelope prior to core collapse. 50+ solar masses is a lot, but if it has a mass of 350 solar masses, losing another 75 solar masses still leaves it with 275 solar masses. That’s more than enough to hit the pair instability.
LBV stars turned out to be older than Wolf-Rayet stars, proving that they are not their progenitors. My understanding is that LBVs are instead believed to come from binary evolution.
So what is the current understanding? I’m looking for a cohesive summary here. There are a ton of scientific articles on the subject, but they are more like bits and pieces than a coherent big picture that a non-astrophysicist can understand. I’m familiar with the general principles but not details!
