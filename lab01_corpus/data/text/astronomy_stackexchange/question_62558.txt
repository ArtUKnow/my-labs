Is it possible to estimate star distance using naked eye and human body only?
Is it possible to estimate star distance using the naked eye using only the human body?
I was hypothesizing one could either cover the star with an arm hair or perhaps measure using some comparison of the smallest distance one can see using visual acuity (which is about $4.8 - 40 um$).
I did some rough math on estimating distance of an object using the naked eye as follows and the look at some later results of an extended equation I developed for distant stars.
$s_{01} = \frac {L_0}{L_1} \frac {D_0}{D_1}$
where $s_{01}$ is the scalar to object $1$ from object at point $0$. $L_0$ is the length of object $0$ and $D_0$ is the distance you measured the length at. This creates an inverse proportion. I ask a question about this formula in this thread.
I then use a correction factor to arrive at some experimental results and that of the nautical and land mile.
Finding the Distance with small magnification changes
$d_1 = sin(\frac {\pi}{2} log_{k_1}(s_{01}\cdot10^2)) \cdot s_{01}$
where $k_1 = 10^{17}$ and then a correction factor to establish miles both nautical and land. As follows $corr_{k_2}(d_1) = d_2 = d_1^2 \cdot k_2 + d_1$. Where $k_{2_{LM}} = \frac {62} {64} \frac {1}{814}$ for land mile and $k_{2_{NM}} = \frac {107}{100} \frac {1}{894}$ for nautical mile. Where $814 = \frac {60}{64} \frac {\sqrt 3}{2} 10^3$ and $814 = \frac {66}{64} \frac {\sqrt 3}{2} 10^3$. (Curious to the history of these small correction factors). Noting small correction factors for some reason on such units of measurement. Then $d_2$ is the corrected distance between two points.
Some experimental tests
I tested this experimentally in the room for some small (< 6) measurements and the results seem to hold on distance. I then checked land and nautical miles as follows and experimentally outside and they seem to also hold:
$L_{mi_{d_{01}}} = \frac {35 cm}{200 um} \approx \frac {14"}{\frac {1}{128} "} = \frac A B$, where $A$ = width of human waist or shoulder width and $B$ = diameter of adult male arm hair both at $35 cm$ focal distance from eye. Then $corr_{k_{2_{LM}}}(L_{mi_{d_{01}}}) \approx 1609 m$ or about $179$ paces. Similarly for the nautical miles $NM_{mi_{d_{01}}} = \frac {35 cm}{180 um} = \frac A B$ noting a small change in arm hair diameter. Then $corr_{k_{2_{NM}}}(L_{mi_{d_{01}}}) \approx 1854 m$. Overall result is if you can place an arm hair over something that looks like a human, you can measure visually a mile. You can also use other body parts like skin folds by scaling in the $d_01$ equation.
I would suspect a star has to be extremely large to see it and also energy wise follow the inverse square law for light reaching the eye. I also think $k_1$ would need to be larger for extreme distances as I only tested over a nautical mile.
Testing on Stars using improved equation
For a test I use Canis Major and Alpha Centauri which are visible to the naked eye. I use the modified equation (for longer distances) for distance given some scaling factor:
Improved Equation
$R = \frac {D_1} {D_0}$ and $d_1 = R\cdot sin(\frac {\pi} {2} \cdot log_{10^{21}}(R \cdot 10^2)) \cdot m + \sqrt 3 \cdot R \cdot m$.
I assume visual acuity around $1$ arc minute or $V = 4.6 um$ diameter of about 2 rods or cones in human retina.
Star Tests
Canis Major: ${Diameter}=3.61\cdot 10^{19} m$, ${Distance}=1.97 \cdot 10^{12} m$. $R=\frac {Diameter} {RodRetinaDia} = 4\cdot 10^{18}$. Placing into above equation for $d_1 \approx 1\cdot 10^{19}$, which is close to published value of $d_{1_{act}} = 3.61 \cdot 10^{19}$.
For Alpha Centauri: ${Diameter}=1.7\cdot 10^{9} m$, ${Distance}=4.11 \cdot 10^{16} m$. $R=\frac {Diameter} {RodRetinaDia} = 3.69\cdot 10^{14}$. Placing into above equation for $d_1 \approx 6.7\cdot 10^{17}$, which is a power of 10 off from the published value of $d_{1_{act}} = 4.11 \cdot 10^{16}$
