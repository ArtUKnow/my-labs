What evidence is needed to identify solar rotational modulation in an irregularly sampled ground-based time series?
I am an undergraduate mathematics student who has recently begun studying time series obtained from ground-based observations of solar emission. I am trying to understand how to distinguish physically meaningful periodic variations from effects introduced by the observations themselves.
The measurements are irregularly distributed in time. Some days contain many observations, while others have none. I formed a daily series by taking the median of the recorded signal values on each observing day, without interpolating missing days.
I was interested in whether the data contain variations associated with solar rotation. I understand that the apparent recurrence of solar active regions can produce a modulation with a characteristic timescale of approximately 27 days, although the observed signal need not be strictly periodic because solar activity evolves.
I applied a Lomb–Scargle periodogram using the actual observation dates. In two observational datasets from different epochs, I found local spectral maxima around 28–29 days. The peak was more pronounced in one dataset than in the other.
However, I do not consider this a secure detection. The observing intervals are incomplete, the measurements may be temporally correlated, and the instrumental response may depend on the viewing geometry. In preliminary tests using autocorrelated surrogate series, the apparent peak was more convincing when examining a narrow, previously specified range of periods than when accounting for the broader range searched. I have therefore not established that its origin is solar.
I have read about rotational modulation in solar irradiance and the effect of the evolution of active regions on the observed periodicities. In particular, the paper "The solar benchmark: rotational modulation of the Sun reconstructed from archival sunspot records" helped me understand why a single spectral peak need not represent a stable rotation period.
What I still find difficult is determining what evidence would be physically convincing in the case of incomplete ground-based observations.
My question is: what observational evidence would be necessary to interpret a peak around 28 days as modulation caused by solar rotation, rather than as a feature of the sampling pattern or the observing system?
Would agreement with an independently measured solar activity index, including the temporal behaviour of the variations, provide meaningful evidence? I am particularly interested in what such an agreement would need to demonstrate, given the evolution of active regions and the possibility of observational modulation.
I would appreciate a reference to an observational solar-physics study that addresses this distinction.
