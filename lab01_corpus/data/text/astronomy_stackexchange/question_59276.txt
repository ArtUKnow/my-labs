Can you determine Earth's precessional period without using the torque formula?
The Julian calendar was in use from 45 BC to 1582, so for 1626 years. The tropical year to 4 decimal places is 365.2422 days.
The slip rate of the vernal equinox through the Julian calendar is
Slip rate = 365.25 - 365.2422 = .0078 days per year
Days slipped = 1626 years × .0078 days per year = 12.6828 days.
I want to use the 12.6828 days slip over a time of 1626 years to determine Earth's precessional period without knowing it's moment of inertia or angle of tilt of its rotational axis to the ecliptic.
Last night I did exactly that, and came up with 46,800 years. Using the torque formula you supposedly get 25,770 years.
Intuitively the approaches should agree. Hipparchus did it without knowing the torque formula, which proves there's another way to determine it. So my question is:
Can you determine Earth's precessional period knowing that the vernal equinox slipped from the first point of Aries in 45 BC to somewhere in pisces, by roughly 12.5° in 1626 years?

From the star chart Aries is going, i.e. FEBRUARIUS, when Pisces is arriving, and that happens at 1.2 hours. Look perpendicular to the ecliptic at the star at the fish head, that's at 1.2 hours.
Now the Romans switched from a 295.307 day per year lunar calendar to the 365 day per mean year solunar calendar known as the Octaeteris in 412 BC. You can figure that out from the fact that Caesar added 90 days to the calendar in 46 BC, to restore the equinox to the same day of year it was when the Octaeteris was implemented.
1.2 hours / 24 hours per 360° = 18°
Thus the first point of Aries moved 18 degrees in 2412 years.
18°/ 2412 years = .00746 degrees per year, so the period of Earth's precession is
360° / .00746 degrees per year = 48,240 years.
This is basically what I got using the more precise vernal equinox slip of 12.6828 days through the Julian calendar. This confirms my original calculation.
Using the star chart there's a very bright star in the constellation Pegasus whose right ascension is identical to the star tau Pisces at the fish head. Tau Pisces has a right ascension of 1.18333 hours. You can see bright stars 30 minutes after sunset.
1.18333 hours + .5 hours = 1 hour 41 minutes.
That's precisely where Aries ends.
Therefore, on the day of the 412 BC vernal equinox, the Romans observed where on the horizon the sun set, then 30 minutes later they saw Aries had moved to the left of where the sun set the day before. Thus that very bright star in Pegasus was vertically above where the sun set , when it set. Now in the Scott Lidell Greek English lexicon it has:

So now I know why they call spring spring. In 412 BC when the Greeks and Romans instituted the Octaeteris, that very bright star in Pegasus (to spring up) was directly above where the sun set, when it set, on the day of the vernal equinox.
