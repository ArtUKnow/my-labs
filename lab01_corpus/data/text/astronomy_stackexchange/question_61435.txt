What could be the possible reason for a lightcurve of an eclipsing binary showing all the dips of the same size?
I came across a phase-folded light curve of the eclipsing binary CRTS J152019.4+292928, which is classified as such in A. J. Drake et al. 2014, ApJS, 213, 9. Interestingly, the light curve shows two dips of nearly equal depth. The period, estimated from the light curve, is approximately 0.167 days, assuming each dip corresponds to a separate cycle, as both dips are indistinguishable in depth.
I'm trying to understand the physical reason behind this. Here are a few possible explanations I could think of:
Both stars are of similar size and luminosity, producing similar primary and secondary eclipses.
Only one star is significantly luminous (e.g., the companion is a faint object like a brown dwarf), and we’re somehow misinterpreting the light curve.
The observed symmetry is influenced by the system's inclination or some geometric effect (though I’m unsure about this).
It might not be an eclipsing binary at all, and the variability arises from a different mechanism (e.g., pulsating star).
For further context, a machine learning-based classifier from ZTF also identifies this object as an eclipsing binary (ZTF light curve attached below - I think the x-axis should be phase not days.).
