How do astronomers know that two objects appearing near one another are in fact close?
This is something I've been wondering since I started photographing stars at night a month ago.
To observers from Earth other stars appear close to each other if they have similar position on the celestial sphere. But because that doesn't tell us anything about distance, only direction, those stars could be at wildly different distances. Good example of that are constellations whose stars are often totally unrelated to each other and quite far apart - e.g. Belt of Orion.
But same logic can be applied to all images taken with a telescope. Therefore it follows that in all images taken by any telescope (and thus anything we've ever observed) having few stars close by doesn't tell us anything about their actual proximity and position in space.
Thus even if you see something like Orion nebula or Pleiades, in principle you cannot conclude those stars have any connection to each other, and they could, in theory, be separated by tens or hundreds of light years. Am I correct about this? Of course, if you can calculate the distance you obtain a 3D picture of what you are seeing, but I'm essentially wondering how much "2D" view actually reveals.
