From an aircraft at 17,000 ft, which horizon should a planet’s geometric altitude be compared with?
I’m checking whether Jupiter could have been visible from a jet at altitude at a historical date and time, and I’m unsure which “horizon” figure is the right comparison.
Setup
Observer: 41.266° N, 81.345° W, about 5,180 m (17,000 ft) above sea level; ground below about 300 m.
Time: 1954-05-24 03:10 UT.
Jupiter (JPL Horizons, airless): azimuth 305.2°, geometric altitude −3.19°, magnitude −1.9.
Three candidate horizons
Geometric dip of the sea-level horizon from 5,180 m: arccos(R/(R+h)) ≈ 2.31°, i.e. the horizon at −2.31°.
The usual “dip with standard refraction” (k ≈ 0.13): about −2.16° apparent.
A ray trace through the 1976 US Standard Atmosphere, following the line of sight that just grazes the sea-level limb out to the top of the atmosphere: it puts that grazing ray at a true (unrefracted) direction of about −3.05° (−2.9° from 15,000 ft, −3.2° from 19,000 ft).
My reasoning: Jupiter’s altitude is geometric, so it should be compared with figure 3, the true direction of the ray that just clears the limb. On that basis Jupiter, at −3.19°, is about 0.14° below the visible horizon at 03:10 UT, and it sinks about 0.15° a minute. Comparing it with figure 2 instead puts it 1.0° below, which I think mixes a geometric altitude with an apparent horizon.
Questions
Is figure 3 the right comparison for “was the planet above the visible horizon”, with the planet’s altitude taken as geometric?
Does the ground below being about 300 m rather than sea level change this appreciably?
Roughly how faint would Jupiter be along a grazing path like this? I estimate about 30 air masses.
