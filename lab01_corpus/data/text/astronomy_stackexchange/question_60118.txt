Longer explanation:
TIME FROM THE SUN
It is very straightforward to get the time and date the day by using sun because our system of time keeping is based on the relative position of the sun.
Observe the moment when the sun is in at it's highest (this gives you the time)
Observe the constellation where the sun is when it sets (this gives you the date).
(It is true that both of these have some error as the solar noon is not directly at 12 o'clock and the exact day of the month can be difficult to pin point from the position of the sun. Technically by memorizing the times when the sun sets during each day you can infer the time directly from the constellation in which the sun is.)
--
TIME FROM THE MOON
However the same thing can be more achieved during the night when the moon is visible. Because if you get
the position. Preferably in which constellation the moon is which translates to Right ascension and declination
phase (full moon, waning or waxing)
the orientation of the phase (in which direction the crescent or gibbous of the moon is pointing)
you can deduce the constellation of where the sun is.
(EDIT: if you cannot see the constellation of the moon then you can use Azimuth and Altitude to calculate Right Ascension and Declination which can yield the constellation in which the moon is. However the easier method is just to see the constellation the moon is in)
--
For example: if the moon is at it's first quarter then sun is is (about) 90 degrees (I don't know the correct value) to the direction the illuminated side is pointing.
And assuming you can see the constellation where moon you can calculate the constellation where the sun is with a star chart.
From there you have the date (which constellation the sun is) and with that you also get the time from the stars (position of the stars gives you a somewhat accurate estimate of the time when the date is known).

What bugs me is that this information seems to not be available in the internet.
Many suggest that to use moon as a clock you need to know the date, but as I explained, you can get the date from the moon too. Similarly some suggest that by knowing the phase and the position of the moon you can deduce the time. This comes from the fact that a full moon is highest at midnight and the first quarter moon is highest at sunset. So by knowing the date you know, for example, when the sun roughly sets and therefore from the position and phase of the moon you know the time.
Adding to this as listed in the comments by Greg Miller it is possible to use precalculated tables (ephemeris https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephemeris) which contain the positions of celestial objects (such as moon) with the date of the position. So with these you can deduce the date by only knowing the position (and probably the phase too) of the moon in the sky. This is because as the Moon's orbit is not always the same but it changes slightly from year to year and is very seldom the exact same.
I argue that this is more complicated system to deduce the time as it requires the knowledge of the tables. I would be interested in learning a way without using tables and where you could do it more independently using only the star chart and the moon which is seen.
Of course you still need to have the star chart or to have memorized it to derive the sun's constellation by looking at the moon, but it is more simple (to me at least)
But indeed a answer to the question is to search through ephemeris like JPL DE (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory_Development_Ephemeris) like Greg Miller suggested.
