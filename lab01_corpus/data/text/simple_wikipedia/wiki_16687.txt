Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343–25 October 1400) was an English writer, poet, and philosopher. He is most famous for writing Canterbury Tales, his estate satire about a group of pilgrims traveling from London to Canterbury and telling tales to pass the time.
Early life
Chaucer was probably born in London. His father, John, and grandfather were prosperous wine merchants in London. The name "Chaucer" is French and means shoemaker. However, not many details are known of his early life and education.
By 1357, Chaucer was a page to Elizabeth, Countess of Ulster, wife of Lionel, 1st Duke of Clarence. In 1360, he was captured by the French near Reims during a battle in the Hundred Years' War. He was then ransomed and released. King Edward III gave £16 towards his release. He is most famous for one his final works, a collection of stories in verse called the Canterbury Tales. His other major work is his Trojan epic, Troilus and Criseyde. His other writings include The Book of the Duchess, The House of Fame, Anelida and Arcite, The Parliament of Fowls, and the Legend of Good Women, as well as many short poems. Though much of his work includes some adaptation and translation, Chaucer's direct translations are his Boece, a translation of the Consolation of Philosophy, and his partial translation of the Romaunt of the Rose. He also wrote A Treatise on the Astrolabe, an instruction manual on the astronomical instrument of the same name dedicated to his son.
Chaucer could read Latin, French, and Italian, and he may have written in those languages himself (though we have no evidence of this), but because he wrote primarily in English, he was esteemed by his contemporaries and near-contemporaries as one of great English poets. He is still sometimes referred to as "the father of English poetry".
