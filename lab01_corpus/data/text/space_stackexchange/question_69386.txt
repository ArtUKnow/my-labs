Can a large extendable heat shield be used for efficient re-entry?
I was wondering why aerospace engineers still have not ideated a way to produce heat shields which are larger in surface area so that a spacecraft during re-entry can aerobrake more efficiently as well as experiencing less heat.
Obviously, a large heat shield requires light but heat-resistant material. And for this, I have taken the sintered silica fibres used in the heat tiles of starships (let me know if there are other light materials that are used or can be used in heat shields). Next, an assembly of this material in a large, appropriately flat, square can be placed underneath the spacecraft that has to re-enter. The assembly, of course, has to be in a way where it is flexible enough to get extended (like a solar panel).
The folded heat shield can work like a solar panel underneath the spacecraft where it can remain in a folded position when not appropriate for use during a mission. When the spacecraft has to re-enter, it can extend this heat shield using a strong pneumatic system that can withstand aerobraking.
Theoretically, could a heat shield engineered like this be feasible to use? I assume that the larger surface area of the heat shield can promise an air resistance factor many magnitudes bigger than regular re-entering spacecrafts. Not to mention the drag experienced by the spacecraft ensures a slower acceleration and more probability that the heat shield does not succumb to drag forces. I also assume that a heat shield made of this material and with a way larger surface area can distribute frictional heat more efficiently than other heat shields which could reduce re-entry failures and the temperature experienced inside the spacecraft.
Would a concept like this be theoretically possible? If so, why hasn't anyone attempted it?
