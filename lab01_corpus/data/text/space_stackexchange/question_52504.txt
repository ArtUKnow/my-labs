Per this answer to Are quasicrystals common on the Moon and Mars?, Curiosity has an X-ray diffractometer.
X-ray diffraction from 3D crystals can (begin to) be understood by embracing Bragg's law. A 3D periodic lattice has many $hkl$ planes that can "Bragg scatter" into specific combinations of directions.
X-ray diffractometers come in several flavors, two of which are scanning diffractometers with many degrees of freedom for the sample orientation and the beam and detector apertures:
Sources clockwise from top left: 1, 2, 3, 4
...and position sensitive area detectors where you control the orientation of the incident beam and sample but collect scattered X-ray flux over a wide range of outgoing directions on a 2D position sensitive detector, which were historically photographic plates.
Sources: left, right
If you have one single crystal sample, analysis can be fairly easy because you only get Bragg reflections when the Bragg conditions are satisfied to within some very small angular range depending on the narrowness of the beam energy and slits and quality and temperature of the crystal.
But often found samples on the ground are polycrystalline, and if the crystallites are smaller than your beam spot size you get a more complicated spot pattern, and if they are really small or you've got a powder-like soil sample you ultimately get lumpy rings from powder X-ray diffraction.
