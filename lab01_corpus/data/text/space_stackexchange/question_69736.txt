Jerk in reentry simulations
These plots are from my own Python-based reentry simulator, visualized in ParaView’s plot-matrix view — which explains the unconventional axis labels. For both plots time moves from right-to-left, with high altitude on the right. For the plot on the right, the vertical axis = jerk in m/s³.
For clarity the reentry i used was for an ATS Shuttle Profile based on the following R_PLANET = 6371000, g_surface = 9.81, H_struct = 8500. Entry params entry altitude = 120000 m, entry_velocity = 7600 m/s entry angle_deg = 2, object C_d = 0.4 A + 260.0 m^2, mass = 104000 eta = o.6 rupture threshold = 2e7 and this was modeled as a dead stick flight
Most reentry simulations I've seen use thresholds based on altitude, Mach number, or angle of attack to transition between phases (e.g., from ballistic to glide).
But what about jerk (the third derivative of position)? Has anyone explored using jerk as a trigger condition in reentry models?
For example: during atmospheric descent, the rate of change of acceleration may spike as plasma drag builds or as structural stresses accumulate. In a simulation I'm working on, I've used jerk to successfully trigger a glide phase emergently—no hardcoded altitude or Mach threshold.
This gave me some surprisingly realistic behavior, including rupture events and terminal velocity overshoots.
Is jerk ever used in professional or academic reentry modeling? Or is it considered too noisy or unstable to be useful?
I'd love any references, criticisms, or anecdotes on how higher derivatives are (or aren’t) used in real spacecraft simulation environments.
