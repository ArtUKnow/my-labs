When should a rocket launch in order to arrive in a particular inclined orbital plane?
We often discuss needing to launch at the right time to enter the correct orbital plane. Webcasts for ISS launches often mention "we have to launch at exactly the moment that the launch site passes under the orbital plane of the ISS".
I am wondering if this is accurate. To me, you should have to launch a bit earlier than that.
For the sake of discussion, let's simplify and say we are launching from the equator, and intending to reach the ISS. Assume we are launching during a "descending pass", where the ISS is traveling from northwest to southeast over the launch site. The launch site, being on the equator, will be near the descending node of the orbit.
Using the standard conventions for heading (positive clockwise, 0 north), the ISS will be flying overhead with a heading of 141 degrees (51 degrees relative to the 90 degrees of straight east along the equator). To join its orbital plane, we will need to also make our velocity vector face 141 degrees.
At the moment of launch, our velocity vector comes from the rotation of the Earth and is facing 90 degrees. When we launch, we will be pushing the velocity vector southward (while also increasing its magnitude). Throughout the launch, the heading angle will increase continuously until it hits 141 degrees.
But that doesn't happen until about 8 minutes after launch. If we launch when the ISS plane and the launch site perfectly align, then over the course of that 8 minutes, we will have flown east for a while before hitting the right inclination. This represents a displacement in RAAN and a failure to match planes.
Ultimately, we should have to launch a bit early, so that our during-launch eastward drift brings us up to the plane by the time we are flying at 141 degrees.
Is this understanding correct? I am particularly interested in knowing if there are any textbooks or other resources discussing this problem of launch timing (and other details of the launch process) for targeting a particular destination orbit.
