What is the optimum lunar latitude for a solar farm and hydrolysis plant?
Water is hypothesized to be present at the lunar poles. There have been proposals to hydrolyzed it into propellant. Large amounts of electrical power will be needed at the location of the hydrolysis plant. Solar panels are a leading contender for this job, along with nuclear and solar thermal https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100017257/downloads/20100017257.pdf
However, the optimum location for solar farms is not at the latitude of the hypothesized water. Should the electricity be transmitted to the water or the water to the solar farm? At what latitude should they be located?
High latitude solar farms require a large pitch distance (the distance between rows of panels) to avoid one panel row from shading the next.

Low latitude
The pitch distance increases with the tangent of the latitude https://azsolarcenter.org/pv-row-to-row-spacing , so farm area increases dramatically near the poles. Low panel density increases the cost of structural supports and conductors.
How to bring the water together with the electricity? Power can be transmitted long distances with high voltage DC current, but this requires significant infrastructure. Transporting water equivalent lunar distances would be a challenge.
The optimum solution depends on the latitude of the solar farm(s) and their number, since 24/7 hydrolysis would require multiple farms.
This discussion ignores the fact that propellant needs to eventually be at the same location as the rocket which needs a fill-up.
As early as 1837, it was suggested there may exist a “peak of eternal light” at high latitude on mountainous astronomic bodies such as the moon. It has been suggested solar panels could be located at these "points of perpetual sunlight".

Electrolysis of water is energy intensive and requires very large solar arrays to provide the necessary 286kJ/mole of water. To fill the equivalent of a Saturn V second stage (total 306,000kg propellant) in a day would require 366,000m^2 of panels if the panels were located on Earth with 12 hour days. This would require a wall 10m high and 36,600 meters long. There are 4 peaks near the lunar South Pole which are illuminated more than 80% of the time, so the equivalent lunar installation may get away with only 22 km of panels. However, the peaks in question are 2 orders of magnitude too small. And the tangent formula for panel spacing still applies, even for rotating panels, since rotating panels can shade their neighbors as effectively as fixed panels.
