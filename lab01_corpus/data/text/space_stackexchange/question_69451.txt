Have vertex-reinforced, polygon section strut designs (like a blackberry stalk) been used in aerospace design?
Hollow circular cross section struts have excellent specific stiffness. They are mathematically optimum for isotropic bending resistance. They are widely used, as on these Space Shuttle external tank mounts:
https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070008105/downloads/20070008105.pdf
However, struts with polygon cross section may outperform a smooth tube for anisotropic loading by resisting local buckling, especially if the vertexes are reinforced. This design is used in nature, as in Blackberry stalks. They could be described as hexagonal cross section, foam core and vertex reinforced cantilevered struts.
.
Biomimetic engineering has given the aerospace industry airplane wings and honeycomb laminates. Is there any use of polygon section struts? The stringer reinforcing inside a fuselage is a close example.
Edit: At Organic Marble’s request I went on an unsuccessful search for evidence of a mass saving for polygon vs circular section under non-isotropic bending loads.
However, informal observation of actual blackberry stalks was interesting. They are tapered, cantilevered beams loaded at the tip (like a fishing rod). The youngest portion (the tip) is round. Slightly older and thicker sections have developed a hexagonal section, but it is randomly oriented. Mature, thick stocks are hexagonal with two vertexes aligned vertically, in the plane of the anisotropic load. Horizontal stalks develop marked vertex ridges whereas vertical stalks (which are isotropicly loaded) retain their youthful round section.
The apparent development of optimized load-bearing reinforcement is consistent with thigmomorphogenesis. I didn’t make up that word … there is even a Wikipedia article on it !!!
Thigmomorphogenesis involves plants altering their development in response to mechanical stimuli. It is a complex process involving signal transduction, mechanosensitive ion channels,
hormonal regulation and transcription regulation.
Presumably this complex mechanism was selected for because it enhanced survival and economized structure.
Aeronautic engineers have similar incentives to economize structure and enhance survival. Have vertex-reinforced hexagonal structures been used in aerospace design ?
