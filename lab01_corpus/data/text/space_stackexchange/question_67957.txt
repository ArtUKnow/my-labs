Should astronauts have cataract surgery before departing on the first Mars missions?
Space travel, especially beyond LEO, increases the risk of cataracts. Insufficient information is available to predict the frequency of disabling cataract formation during a Mars mission. If disabling cataracts developed amongst the crew before return, it would pose a significant hazard. Clear Lens Extraction (CLE) with lens implant (cataract surgery without a cataract) is safe (compared with space travel at least), and ISS astronauts have been on missions following this surgery without problems. On balance of risks, is it safer to require all astronauts to have CLE before the initial Mars missions?
A cataract is a clouding of the lens inside the eye. There are several types of cataracts depending on the location of the cloudiness:
Nuclear sclerosis is diffuse clouding of the core of the lens. It is the most common pattern in “old age” cataract.
Cortical cataracts are spoke-shaped opacities which start in the periphery of the lens and gradually extend towards the center where they block vision. Common in astronaut cataracts.
Posterior Subcortical cataracts are small and dense. They form at the posterior (back) pole of the lens. Because they are close to the optical node of the lens, they have the most deleterious effect on vision of the three common cataracts. They also tend to form rapidly. They are associated with radiation exposure, both ionizing radiation and IR.
The lens is a very unusual tissue. It is actually older than the person who owns that eye because the fetal nucleus was formed before the person was born. It has extremely low oxygen partial pressure because it has no blood vessels. It is dependent for oxygen and nutrients on the slow circulation of aqueous humor. This circulation is provided by thermal currents due to the cornea (front of the eye) being colder than the interior of the eye. This thermal circulation does not occur in microgravity. The lens has little or no capacity to heal damage: traumatic, thermal, hypoxic or radiation.
Space travel increases the risk of cataracts. Although this is often blamed on radiation exposure, there is evidence it may be multifactoral. Animal models of radiation-induced cataracts associate radiation with subcapsular cataracts, but space travel seems to be associated with cortical cataracts. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17511300
U.S. Air Force and U.S. Navy aviators' cataracts were most commonly
located in the posterior subcapsular region of the lens while
astronauts' cataracts were most likely to originate in the cortical
zone.
There is evidence that the lens needs a “Goldilocks” concentration of oxygen (not too high, not too low). https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S001448352200272X
An important radiobiology tenet is the oxygen effect where hypoxic
tissues have up to three-fold lower radiosensitivity to DNA damage or
cell death compared to well-oxygenated cells … The oxygen effect is
particularly relevant to sparsely ionizing particles such as
high-energy protons, the most abundant type of charged particle in
deep space, distant from the Earth's surface. High density ionization
of body tissues by heavy ions – which are less sensitive to the oxygen
effect than low density ionizing radiation such as protons – only
contributes about 1% of the galactic cosmic radiation dose received by
astronauts …. postulated that even a small increase in the oxygen
tension of hypoxic ocular tissues results in a relatively large change
in radiosensitivity or OER from space radiation.
Even though maintaining hypoxia is critical for lens health, and
intraocular oxygen level changes are an important aspect of
cataractogenesis, current in vitro research involving LECs (Lens
Epithelial Cells) seldom takes these factors into account, and
irradiation studies, even less so.
Higher than normal ocular oxygen levels are linked to nuclear cataract
and lower oxygen levels are possibly associated with PSC and/or
cortical cataract.
Recent estimates of the daily space radiation exposure on the far side
of the Moon's surface (1369 μSv/day) are ∼1.8-fold higher than the
dose rate acquired on the International Space Station, which in turn
is typically ∼210-fold higher than the exposure to natural ionizing
radiation sources on Earth … The radiation dose on a 3-year mission
to Mars would be high (∼1 Sv) and could increase several-fold if
astronauts are exposed to intense solar particle events… However,
there is no definitive quantification on the latent period of cataract
progression due to space radiation, especially at the moderate or high
doses accumulated during prolonged missions. Although assessments of
atomic bomb survivors in the first five years after detonation have
severe limitations, those exposed to acute doses >1 Sv (note that
astronauts are chronically exposed to different types of ionizing
radiation) may have a cataractogenic latency as short as 2–3 years.
Therefore, research is needed to assess strategies (e.g., to reduce
ocular oxidative stress) to determine and lengthen the latent period
of space-radiation induced cataract development.
Since the exact risk and effectiveness of mitigation strategies will remain unknown until we have a significant body of evidence from long term deep space travel, is it not prudent to perform pre-emptive cataract surgery on flight-essential astronauts until this evidence is available?
