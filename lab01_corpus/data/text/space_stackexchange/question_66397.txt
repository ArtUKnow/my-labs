TWR of near-term NTR engines
With DRACO aiming for on-orbit demonstration of nuclear thermal rocket propulsion (NTR) in 2027, it seems like a technology that has had on-and-off interest for the last 70 years might finally become a reality.
It's pretty well established that such engines would have an amazing (compared to chemical rockets) ISP of $900-1000$s. This is set by using hydrogen as the reaction mass (best choice due to having the highest specific heat capacity) and operating at the maximum temperature that the nuclear reactor can have without melting, tearing itself to pieces through heat stress, or being eroded by the hydrogen flowing through it.
But what's the thrust-to-weight (TWR) ratio of such engines? I've tried to find stuff in the literature, but without much success. Wikipedia claims (sadly without source) that the NERVA program that investigated this in the 50s and 60s eventually reached a TWR of $7$. But this 2009 design that uses a NERVA-type engine for a manned mission to Mars uses an engine TWR of only $3.43$. I couldn't find anything at all about the TWR the DRACO program was aiming for. Even this very recent book on NTR propulsion doesn't mention it at all - at least not in the parts of the book I could access.
The main innovations, AFAIK, of the new NTRs is that they use less highly enriched Uranium (~20% rather than ~90%) for safety, cost, and legal reasons; as well as encasing the fuel inside the reactor in ceramic compounds (rather that metallic ones). I'd guess that the former would lower TWR and the latter increase it? But nuclear engineering is sufficiently complex that I'm not confident at all about those guesses, let alone how big an impact they would have. Then there is also the general progress in material science and construction techniques that I would assume would allow for lighter construction now than in the 60s.
If there are any theoretical calculations about what TWR ratios a near-term NTR engine might achieve, or any documents describing what has actually been reached (or is being aimed for), I'd be delighted to see them.
