Why doesn't differential radiation pressure cause objects in space to spin faster and faster forever?
Imagine an asteroid, or a piece of space debris. In the general case, it is unlikely that the solar radiation pressure it is exposed to will be perfectly balanced across its surface. In the extreme, we can imagine something that looks like a Crooks Radiometer assembly (which, to be clear, does not primarily operate on radiation pressure but is instead a heat engine working on gas). If an asteroid is even slightly lighter or darker on one side (by which I mean clockwise or counter clockwise), even micronewtons will add up over millions of years. What dissipative force prevents these pressures from building into an ever-increasing rotation rate? The best I can think of is gravity gradient, but I'm not sure if that is a full explanation since it isn't fundamentally dissipative in nature.
